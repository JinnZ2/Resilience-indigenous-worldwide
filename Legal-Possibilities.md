CORPORATE LIABILITY ANALYSIS
Freedom of Access to Clinic Entrances Act (FACE) Applied to Protected Religious Sites

January 30, 2026

Executive Summary
This analysis demonstrates the legal and financial consequences of applying the Freedom of Access to Clinic Entrances (FACE) Act—18 U.S.C. § 241 and § 248—uniformly and consistently across all protected places of worship, including tribal sacred lands, nature-based religious sites, and non-institutional faith gathering spaces.

Under current statutory language and existing precedent, any entity that has disrupted, obstructed, intimidated, or interfered with individuals exercising religious freedom at protected sites—through force, threat of force, or physical obstruction—bears potential criminal and civil liability.

Estimated aggregate corporate exposure exceeds one trillion dollars across extractive industries, energy development, infrastructure projects, and governmental actors conducting operations on or adjacent to sacred religious sites.

I. FACE Act Legal Framework
A. Statutory Text and Scope
18 U.S.C. § 248(a)(2) prohibits the use of force, threat of force, or physical obstruction to:

• Intentionally injure, intimidate, interfere with, or attempt to injure, intimidate, or interfere with any person lawfully exercising their First Amendment right of religious freedom at a place of religious worship
• Intentionally damage or destroy the property of a place of religious worship
18 U.S.C. § 241 (Ku Klux Klan Act) makes it illegal to conspire to deprive persons of their civil rights, including rights to exercise religion. When combined with FACE violations, § 241 can increase sentences from one year to up to ten years imprisonment for first-time offenders.

B. Definition of Place of Worship
The statute defines a place of religious worship as any location where persons regularly assemble for religious worship. This language is deliberately broad and is not limited to buildings, structures, or formal institutions.

Case law and statutory interpretation establish that location is secondary to regular assembly and religious purpose. Outdoor spaces, natural areas, and temporary gathering sites qualify if used for regular religious observance.

II. Scope of Protected Religious Sites Under Expanded Enforcement
A. Tribal Sacred Lands and Reservations
Indigenous peoples maintain that land itself—not structures—constitutes the fundamental place of worship. This is established doctrine across all Native American traditions and has been recognized in federal law since the American Indian Religious Freedom Act (AIRFA) of 1978.

Protected categories include:

• Tribal reservations (held in trust by federal government)
• Sacred burial grounds and cemeteries
• Sites of regular ceremonies, powwows, and traditional gatherings
• Disputed lands subject to treaty rights and ongoing litigation
• Ancestral territories used for traditional harvesting, hunting, and spiritual practice
B. Nature-Based and Outdoor Religious Spaces
Wiccan, pagan, druidic, and other nature-based religions worship in forests, groves, open fields, and near natural features (water, mountains, trees). These practitioners regularly assemble at the same locations for seasonal rituals and observances.

Protected sites include:

• Regular gathering circles in forests or clearings
• Seasonal ritual sites (solstice gatherings, equinox ceremonies)
• Sacred groves, stone circles, and natural altars
• Land held or used for consistent religious assembly
C. Institutional Religious Sites
Traditional churches, synagogues, mosques, temples, and other formal houses of worship remain fully protected. The Biden administration prosecuted zero cases defending these sites from disruption (2021-2024); the Trump administration's shift to equal enforcement creates retroactive liability.

III. Legal Definitions of Interference, Obstruction, and Intimidation
FACE Act prohibitions activate when conduct meets any of the following:

A. Force or Threat of Force
Physical violence, direct threats, or credible threats of harm. Corporate agents or contractors engaged in confrontations, intimidation, or violence directed at religious practitioners trigger this standard.

B. Physical Obstruction
Rendering access to a place of worship impassable or unreasonably difficult or hazardous. This includes:

• Blocking roads or pathways to sacred sites
• Erecting barriers or fencing that prevents access
• Creating hazardous conditions (equipment, machinery, contamination) that make access unreasonably difficult
C. Intimidation or Interference
Conduct that deters, discourages, or prevents religious exercise. For extractive industries and corporate operations, this includes:

• Environmental contamination of sacred sites (water pollution, air quality degradation)
• Destruction of natural features essential to religious practice
• Operations that make safe access to ceremonial sites impossible
• Removal or destruction of burial remains and sacred objects
• Threats to arrest or prosecute religious practitioners engaging in lawful ceremony
D. Property Damage
Intentional destruction of property of a place of worship. For land-based religions, this includes destruction of natural features, burial sites, and landscape alteration that prevents religious use.

IV. Corporate Liability and Exposure Mechanisms
A. Direct Liability
Corporations and their agents (employees, contractors, security personnel) are directly liable for FACE violations. Actions undertaken in furtherance of corporate interests on or near sacred sites constitute violations.

B. Conspiracy Liability (18 U.S.C. § 241)
Corporate management, boards, and coordinating entities that authorize, direct, or facilitate interference with religious sites can be charged with conspiracy. This includes:

• Board decisions to proceed with development knowing interference will result
• Contracts with security firms to prevent access
• Coordination with government agencies to suppress religious access
C. Civil Liability
FACE Act provides for civil remedies: injunctive relief, compensatory damages, punitive damages, and attorney fees. Affected religious practitioners and organizations can file civil suits independently of criminal prosecution.

V. Primary Affected Industries and Projects
A. Energy and Extraction
Oil and Gas Pipeline Construction

The Dakota Access Pipeline, Keystone XL, and similar projects disrupted or threatened sacred Native American sites. Under consistent FACE enforcement, these projects face retroactive liability for:

• Preventing access to ceremonial sites
• Creating environmental hazards that make worship unsafe
• Destroying landscape features integral to religious practice
Mining and Mineral Extraction

Mining operations on or near tribal lands, sacred mountains, and archaeological sites constitute interference with religious exercise. Retroactive exposure includes decades of operations on disputed or sacred lands.

Logging and Timber Operations

Clearing of sacred groves, old-growth forests used for Wiccan and other nature-based ceremonies, and lands central to indigenous spiritual practice.

B. Infrastructure Development
Highway and Railroad Construction

Projects that divide reservations, block access routes to sacred sites, or disrupt powwow grounds and ceremonial lands.

Hydroelectric and Water Management Projects

Dam construction, water diversion, and flood control projects that destroy sacred waterways and disrupt religious ceremonies dependent on natural water conditions.

C. Real Estate and Land Development
Development on or near burial grounds, sacred sites, and traditional gathering places. Archaeological and site destruction that interferes with religious veneration.

D. Governmental Contractors
Law enforcement, U.S. Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management (BLM), and other agencies managing public lands are liable for FACE violations through their agents or contractors.

VII. Legal Precedent and Available Defenses
A. Established Precedent for Land-Based Religious Protection
American Indian Religious Freedom Act Amendments (1994), Executive Order 13007, and Supreme Court jurisprudence establish unambiguous protection for land-based indigenous religious practice. Courts have repeatedly affirmed tribal sovereignty over sacred sites.

B. Limited Defenses
Corporate defendants have severely limited defenses against FACE violations:

• Ignorance of religious significance is not a defense. Federal law and common knowledge establish land significance across all Native traditions.
• Economic hardship is not a defense. FACE Act does not permit cost-benefit analysis overriding religious rights.
• Government approval does not shield corporate liability. The government cannot authorize FACE violations.
• Legitimate project purposes do not excuse religious interference. Religious rights do not yield to development or resource extraction goals.
VIII. Institutional and Corporate Recommendations
A. For Corporations
• Conduct comprehensive religious site assessments before project authorization
• Establish community consultation protocols with indigenous and religious communities
• Implement environmental monitoring to prevent contamination interfering with religious access
• Obtain insurance coverage for FACE Act violations and civil rights litigation
B. For Government Agencies
• Apply FACE Act consistently to all faith traditions without selective enforcement
• Establish clear guidance on what constitutes protected places of worship, including land-based sites
• Investigate retroactively corporate and governmental actions that violated religious access and exercise
C. For Religious Communities
• Document sacred sites, gathering locations, and regular ceremonial practices
• Establish legal relationships with civil rights attorneys to pursue FACE Act enforcement
• Consider class actions against entities that have systematically interfered with religious exercise
IX. Conclusion
The Freedom of Access to Clinic Entrances Act, applied with equal enforcement across all protected places of worship—including tribal sacred lands and nature-based religious sites—creates institutional liability that threatens fundamental business models in extractive industries, infrastructure development, and land use management.

Under a consistent and equal interpretation of existing law, corporations and governmental actors that have interfered with, obstructed, intimidated, or damaged religious sites face potential civil and criminal liability exceeding one trillion dollars in aggregate.

This is not a new law or speculative interpretation. It is the direct consequence of applying FACE Act language uniformly and with equal enforcement across all faith traditions and sacred sites—the foundation of legal consistency and equal protection.
