# The Count Was Never About Immigration

The current fight over who the census counts gets framed as an immigration story. It isn't. It's a **legibility** story — *who is visible to the count* — and that mechanism has always hit Indian Country hardest.

## The mechanism, not the headline

You don't have to change the law to change the outcome. You only have to change who answers.

Suppress the response rate in a community and you get an undercount — no rule change, no amendment, no court fight. The official method stays intact. The people just disappear from the total. The immigration debate is the *visible* lever. The undercount is the *general* one, and it runs on the same parts:

- distrust of the federal count
- addresses that don't fit the standard grid (rural, reservation, non-postal)
- remote geography and thin enumeration
- records-based tagging that misses whoever isn't already in the records
- chilling effect — a single hostile question drops response rates in the targeted community

Every one of those already describes reservation enumeration. It's not a hypothetical for Native communities. It's the standing condition.

## What the count actually controls

One number drives two separate systems:

- **Representation** — apportionment (House seats → Electoral College weight) *and* intrastate redistricting
- **Funding** — the share of ~$1.5 trillion/yr in federal allocation: Indian Health Service, tribal housing, roads, schools

Undercount isn't a statistical footnote. It's a direct cut to services *and* a dilution of political weight, locked in for ten years, with no law changed.

## The number

American Indians and Alaska Natives living on reservations were the **most undercounted group in the 2020 Census — 5.6%**, up from 4.9% in 2010. Highest of any racial or ethnic group, both decades. Persistent, not accidental.
